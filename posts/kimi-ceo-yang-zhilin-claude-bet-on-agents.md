# Kimi's CEO: Claude Didn't Win on Reasoning — It Bet Everything on Agents

*And what that bet means for anyone who uses AI at work.*

In a wide-ranging interview on the Zhang Xiaojun Podcast, Yang Zhilin — founder and CEO of Moonshot AI, the company behind Kimi — offered one of the clearest explanations yet of where AI is actually heading. A clip from that conversation has been making the rounds, and its headline claim sounds provocative: **Claude didn't win because it reasons better. It won because it bet on agents.**

But the real story is more interesting than the headline. Here's what Yang actually said, and why it matters even if you've never written a line of code.

## Two ways to make AI smarter

Yang explains that there are two fundamentally different ways to make an AI more capable while it's working on your task:

1. **Think harder.** The AI sits quietly and reasons through the problem in its "head" before answering — like a student working through a math problem on scratch paper.
2. **Do more.** The AI acts in the world — searching the web, opening files, running code, checking results — over many rounds, until the job is done. This is what makes an AI an **agent**.

His memorable image for pure reasoning: a *brain in a vat* — a brain floating in a fish tank, cut off from the world, able only to think. An agent is that same brain, but with **hands**.

Claude's maker, Anthropic, bet early on the second path. Its models weren't the strongest pure reasoners, but they became exceptionally good at *doing* — using tools, working in loops, completing multi-step tasks. That bet paid off.

## The catch: a great agent still needs a great brain

Here's the part the viral headline misses. Yang's point wasn't that the agent bet was wrong — it was that it has a ceiling:

> "If you want to build the *best* agent — one that solves the most complex tasks — you ultimately need reasoning to be just as strong. It's not a required order in research, but to make agents the best, you also need to make reasoning the best."

In other words: fancy tools and clever packaging around a mediocre brain only get you so far. The quality of the underlying model — the brain itself — is what determines how far the agent can go. That's why Kimi pours its energy into the base model.

## The next leap: AI that builds AI

Yang's most striking concrete goal: **he wants Kimi's current model, K2, to help develop its successor, K3** — proposing ideas, running experiments, analyzing results, iterating. That, he says, is what the industry's "L4" (innovator) stage really means. And it's impossible without agentic skills.

Beyond that lies "L5" — not one agent, but **teams of agents**: one researches, one writes, one checks the work, all in parallel. Early versions of this already exist today.

And is there a final summit? Yang doesn't think so. He compares AI progress to climbing a snow mountain with no top: every problem solved opens new problems — and that's the fun of it. AGI, in his view, is a *direction*, not a destination.

## What this means for you (no technical background needed)

**1. Stop asking AI questions. Start handing it jobs.**
Agents are built to *do* whole tasks: "Research these five competitors and build me a comparison table." "Turn these meeting notes into slides." Give Kimi the goal, not just a prompt.

**2. Describe success, not steps.**
Yang manages his own team the way modern AI is trained: set the goal and define what a good outcome looks like — then let people (or the AI) find the path. Don't micromanage with rigid instructions. Tell Kimi what "done well" means, then give feedback.

**3. Trust, but verify.**
Yang is candid about AI's weak spot: it's best at tasks similar to what it has practiced, and shakier on truly new situations. Let it do the first 80%, then review like you would a capable intern's work — especially for anything high-stakes.

**4. Choose brains over packaging.**
When picking AI tools, the strength of the underlying model matters more than flashy features. A smart wrapper around a weak brain still fails on hard problems.

**5. Keep re-testing your assumptions.**
With models now helping build their own successors, progress is accelerating. Whatever your "AI can't do that" list is, revisit it every few months — tasks that failed last year often just work now.

## The bottom line

AI is shifting from a smart chat box into a tireless colleague that can carry out real, multi-step work. Yang calls AI "an amplifier of human civilization" — and the people who benefit most won't be the most technical ones. They'll be the ones who learn to delegate well: define the goal, set the standard, review the result.

That's a skill worth practicing today.
